What cross-sectional data can and cannot tell you?

 

“As young adults have moved through their twenties over the past decade, their anxiety has steadily worsened. A generation is becoming more anxious as it ages.”

 

- From the National Health Statistics Center.

 

They drew a random sample of about 27,000 adults yearly, each one independent from year to year (no one is surveyed twice). They completed the interview once, asking questions regarding symptoms in the past 30 days. They reported the data for adults aged 18-25, which shows that their anxiety symptoms rose from 8.1% in 2014 to 14.7% in 2023, but they also noted that the survey mode shifted from in-person to a mix of phone and web interviewing in 2019.

 

This claim assumed that as young adults moved through their 20s, they are getting more anxious, but the design never established whether the passage of time caused anxiety to increase or identified what caused that increase in anxiety levels. Also, the interviewers did not follow the same people over the years, so we have no baseline for those interviewed in 2023, and we do not know the trend of anxiety in those interviewed in 2014. This is an issue in a cross-sectional study because, with this study, only a single glance is done. Cross-sectional studies can tell us how common something is, i.e., the anxiety rate, but they cannot tell us what caused the change or why it changed. The authors should have published this instead: “The prevalence of frequent anxiety symptoms among adults aged 18–25 increased from 8.1% in 2014 to 14.7% in 2023, while prevalence among adults aged 50–64 remained relatively stable”.
