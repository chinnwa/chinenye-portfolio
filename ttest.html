Does Your Writing Hand Beat Your Other Hand?

What We Did

Our class conducted an activity to compare how many times students could write "I am amazing" in 30 seconds using their writing hand and their other hand. All nine students wrote the same sentence for the same amount of time with each hand. Each student also answered a Yes or No question about whether they write in script. We performed three t-tests to compare the class's results with our professor's benchmark, compare the Yes and No groups, and compare performance between both hands.

Three T-Test Write-Ups

1. One-Sample T-Test (Class vs. Professor)

The class's average was 6.33, which was 1.67 repetitions lower than the professor's benchmark of 8.00.

The p-value is 0.0011, which is less than 0.05, making the difference statistically significant.

In plain words: Students wrote fewer repetitions than the professor, and the difference is unlikely to be due to chance alone. However, the results do not explain why the professor performed better.

2. Independent-Samples T-Test (Yes vs. No)

Students who answered Yes averaged 6.40 repetitions, slightly higher than the 6.25 average for those who answered No, a difference of 0.15 repetitions.

The p-value is 0.8398, which is greater than 0.05, so the difference is not statistically significant.

In plain words: Students who write in script performed slightly better, but the difference was too small to provide sufficient evidence of a meaningful difference between the groups.

3. Paired-Samples T-Test (Writing Hand vs. Other Hand)

Students averaged 6.33 repetitions with their writing hand compared to 2.56 with their other hand, a difference of 3.78 repetitions.

The p-value is less than 0.001, which is below 0.05, making the difference statistically significant.

In plain words: Students completed more repetitions with their writing hand than with their other hand. However, the results do not establish what caused the difference.

Overall Findings — In Plain Words

Our results showed that students performed better with their writing hand than their other hand, although their average was lower than the professor's benchmark. Both differences were statistically significant, while writing in script showed no statistically significant difference in performance.

What We Would Do Differently

If we repeated this activity, we would make several changes to improve the accuracy and reliability of our findings.

First, we would increase the sample size beyond nine students to obtain more representative results. We would also randomize which hand students use first to reduce the effects of fatigue or practice.

Additionally, we would allow multiple trials for each hand and calculate the average to minimize unusual results. We would standardize the timing, writing materials, and instructions to ensure consistency.

We would also consider students' usual writing styles and include more professors or adults rather than comparing the class to only one person.

Overall, these improvements would reduce potential bias, strengthen the reliability of our data, and allow us to make more meaningful comparisons without assuming causation.
